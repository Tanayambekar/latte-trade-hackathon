Under the hood

How Latte Trade works, end to end

Latte Trade is a hackathon prototype, not a brokerage. The concept in our MochaTrade notes is that a user can gain price exposure to a selected US-listed asset without becoming the legal shareholder. The position is represented through a perpetual-futures-style tracking instrument, while the user funds and settles in INR. Below is the complete journey — what the user does, what the backend is intended to do, what changes in the position value, where fees and risk enter, and what happens when the user exits. Read the twelve steps in order once and the whole model should be clear.

One concrete example

Suppose a user allocates ₹10,000 to track Apple. The user does not become an Apple shareholder through this prototype. Instead, the platform records a synthetic position whose value is designed to follow the referenced Apple price. If the referenced price rises, the position's unrealized value rises; if it falls, the position loses value. In a production perpetual-futures design, margin, funding, fees, liquidation rules and the exact reference-price mechanism must all be disclosed before the user trades.

This is the key distinction: ownership of a stock and price exposure through a derivative are different things. The hackathon concept uses the second model to make global-market exposure accessible without pretending that the user owns the underlying share.

Your starter checklist, before you buy anything

    Long-term capital

    Investment — build the core first

    Investment is the slower, ownership-oriented side of Latte Trade. The objective is to build a diversified long-term portfolio using a repeatable research process rather than trying to predict every short-term price move.

    Risk first: never use emergency money, borrowed money, or money needed for near-term expenses for market speculation. Returns are uncertain, and a loss can be substantial.

    1. Define the goal

    Write the goal, amount, time horizon and maximum acceptable drawdown before choosing a company. A five-year goal and a five-week goal should not use the same risk budget.

    2. Research the business

    Understand revenue sources, margins, debt, cash flow, competition, management, valuation and the reasons the thesis could be wrong. Use primary company filings where possible.

    3. Diversify deliberately

    Do not let one company, sector or theme dominate the portfolio simply because its recent performance looks attractive. Diversification reduces single-position risk; it does not eliminate market risk.

    4. Add gradually

    For beginners, a planned contribution schedule can reduce the temptation to make one large timing decision. The schedule should still be reviewed when the investment thesis changes.

    5. Review the thesis

    Review on a calendar: earnings, debt, cash generation, competitive position and valuation. A falling price alone is not automatically a reason to buy more.

    6. Keep a risk budget

    Separate the core long-term portfolio from a small experimental allocation. If you explore higher-risk strategies, size them so a complete loss would not damage essential financial goals.

    High-risk learning lab

    Perpetual Futures — understand the risk before the leverage

    A perpetual future is a derivative designed to track an underlying asset without a fixed expiry date. You post margin rather than paying the full notional value. That creates leverage: a relatively small price move in the underlying can create a much larger percentage gain or loss on your margin.

    Important: perpetual futures can lose money quickly, including through liquidation. The module below is an educational simulator, not a trading recommendation or a promise that the algorithm can predict prices.

    1. Reference price

    The contract follows a reference price built from an underlying market or index. The exact source, update frequency and fallback rules must be defined.

    2. Margin

    Your collateral supports a larger notional position. Higher leverage means less price movement is needed to materially affect the margin.

    3. Funding

    Perpetual contracts commonly use periodic funding payments to keep contract pricing aligned with the reference market. Funding can be a cost or a receipt depending on the mechanism and position.

    4. Liquidation

    If losses reduce available margin below the required threshold, the position can be forcibly closed. A stop-loss is not a guarantee against gaps or execution differences.

    5. Long vs short

    Long exposure benefits from an increase in the reference price; short exposure benefits from a decrease. Both can lose money when the market moves against the position.

    6. Position sizing

    Calculated risk begins with the rupee amount you are willing to lose, then works backwards to the position size and stop distance. Leverage should never be chosen first.

    Transparent Latte Signal Engine

    This prototype makes the proposed decision process visible instead of presenting a mysterious “AI signal”. In production, each input would be connected to a verified market-data, company-filing and news/sentiment provider. In this offline hackathon prototype, the values below are simulated so the workflow can be demonstrated without pretending to have live internet feeds.

    How the algorithm is intended to work

    1

    Collect

    Pull current price/volume data, company financials and filings, historical price series, macro indicators, recent news and publicly available trader/sentiment indicators. Every data point gets a timestamp and source label.

    2

    Normalize

    Convert different frequencies and scales into comparable signals. Remove duplicate headlines, flag stale data, and separate facts from opinions before scoring.

    3

    Detect historical regimes

    Measure previous spikes, drawdowns, volatility clusters, trend persistence and recovery times. The output is a historical map — not a claim that the same pattern must repeat.

    4

    Score the evidence

    Combine fundamentals, trend, sentiment, volatility and market regime using fixed weights. Weak or conflicting evidence lowers confidence rather than forcing a trade.

    5

    Calculate a risk-defined window

    Instead of saying “buy now”, the engine can display a hypothetical entry zone, invalidation level, maximum rupee loss and review horizon derived from the selected risk budget and historical volatility.

    6

    Explain every output

    The user sees the contributing inputs, weights, timestamps, conflicts and assumptions. If the evidence is stale or contradictory, the correct output is “insufficient confidence”, not a forced position.

    Calculated risk alongside long-term investing

    Core bucket

    Keep the majority of the planned investment allocation in the long-term portfolio. The exact percentage is personal and depends on goals, income, liquidity needs and risk tolerance.

    Experimental bucket

    If the user chooses to learn perpetual futures, define a small, pre-committed risk budget. Never move money from essential goals into the leveraged account after a loss to “win it back”.

    Loss rule

    Choose the maximum rupee loss before opening a trade. Position size = maximum acceptable loss ÷ distance from entry to invalidation, adjusted for fees and contract mechanics.

    Example risk equation: maximum loss = ₹10,000 → stop distance = 2.5% → approximate notional = ₹4,00,000 before fees/funding and execution effects. This is an illustration, not a recommended position size.
